1. INTRODUCTION
---------------
In a Cloud Radio Access Network (C-RAN) the baseband processing of many sites is concentrated in
shared baseband unit (BBU) pools, and each site keeps only a remote radio head (RRH) linked to its
pool by a fronthaul. The aim of the project is to choose the number and the location of the BBU
pools and to assign every RRH to a pool, respecting the maximum delay and balancing the load [3].
The result depends on the scenario, that is, on the position and the density of the RRHs. In this
first stage we define the scenario: we build a dataset of real RRHs for an area of 30 x 30 km from
public data [1] and check it against OpenCelliD [2].


2. SCENARIO
-----------
The scenario is centred on the Santiago Bernabeu stadium in Madrid (40.4531 N, 3.6883 W) and covers
30 x 30 km, from 40.318 N to 40.588 N and from 3.866 W to 3.511 W. We chose it because the real
deployment is well documented and because one box contains very different densities: a dense urban
core, large parks with few sites (Casa de Campo, Monte de El Pardo), the airport, the towns along
the M-40 ring road and a sparse periphery. This makes the placement of the BBU pools non-trivial.


3. DATA ACQUISITION
-------------------
The data comes from Infoantenas [1], the official register of mobile base stations. Its web viewer
requests the stations of the visible map window from a GeoJSON service, which we queried directly
with a bounding box. Each station is returned with its code, operator, address and coordinates, but
without technology or frequency. We divided the scenario into 15 x 15 tiles of 2 x 2 km and sent one
request per tile, 0.5 s apart. The 225 requests returned 4,789 distinct stations (50 tiles are empty,
the busiest has 211 stations). To check that the service does not truncate its answers, we queried
the busiest tile again as 4 and as 16 sub-tiles and obtained the same 211 stations. We stored every
answer unmodified.

The frequency bands of a station are listed on its detail page, so we downloaded the 4,789 detail
pages. A frequency does not identify the radio technology: 700 MHz is used by 4G and by 5G, and 900
and 2100 MHz can carry several generations. We therefore use the bands only as evidence (Table 1).
Stations whose only bands are 700, 900 or 2100 MHz are labelled ambiguous: they are kept in the
dataset, but they are not counted as 4G or 5G.

Table 1. Frequency bands used as evidence of the technology of a station.
  4G evidence   800, 1800 and 2600 MHz
  5G evidence   3.5 GHz and 26 GHz
  Ambiguous     700, 900 and 2100 MHz


4. DATA PROCESSING
------------------
The raw files are never modified. We remove duplicate stations (same operator and code) and label
each station 4G, 5G, 4G+5G or ambiguous according to Table 1. Several operators often share one
tower, so we then merge all stations closer than 50 m, whatever their operator and label, into a
single RRH located at their mean position. The distance is computed in a local plane,
x = (lon - lon0) k_lon and y = (lat - lat0) k_lat, with k_lat = 111.19 km/deg and
k_lon = k_lat cos(lat0) = 84.6 km/deg. The label of an RRH combines the labels of its stations, and
an RRH whose stations are all ambiguous is itself ambiguous. We call core the RRHs with 4G or 5G
evidence. Each RRH keeps its operators, bands and label, a flag in_core, and a field is_synthetic
(false for real sites) that will mark the synthetic RRHs added later. The dataset is stored as JSON
and CSV, and the processing is implemented in Python.


5. RESULTS
----------
The 4,789 stations merge into 2,304 RRHs (Table 2). 2,085 of them are core RRHs: 1,124 with 4G and
5G, 951 with 4G only and 10 with 5G only. The other 219 RRHs (about 10 %) are ambiguous. The
simulations will use the core RRHs, and the ambiguous ones are kept for sensitivity tests. In total
2,075 core RRHs provide 4G and 1,134 provide 5G. By operator, Movistar is present in 1,432 core
RRHs, Orange in 1,067, Vodafone in 1,012, DS Mobile Networks in 241 and MasMovil/Yoigo in 24; the
total is larger than 2,085 because 1,109 RRHs are shared by two or more operators, which confirms
that the merging step is needed. The largest RRH merges 8 stations.

Table 2. Number of elements after each processing step.
  Distinct stations                         4,789
  Stations with 4G or 5G evidence           4,146
  Stations with ambiguous bands only          643
  RRHs after merging (50 m)                 2,304
  core RRHs (4G or 5G evidence)             2,085
    of which 4G + 5G                        1,124
    of which 4G only                          951
    of which 5G only                           10

Figures 1 and 2 show the RRHs and the density of the core RRHs. The density is highest in the
centre of Madrid and decreases towards the periphery, with a ring of intermediate density along the
M-30 and M-40 roads, and the large parks appear as empty areas. The scenario therefore combines
dense and sparse zones, as intended.

6. VALIDATION
-------------
In three areas of about 1 km2 with different density (Salamanca, Alcobendas and Monte de El Pardo)
we checked whether OpenCelliD has an LTE or NR cell within 500 m of each RRH. All 40 RRHs have a
match (28 of 28, 11 of 11 and 1 of 1), so none appears to be invented or misplaced. The test is
limited: the areas are small, the tolerance is easily met where OpenCelliD has many cells, and it
cannot show missing stations. OpenCelliD lists more positions than we have RRHs (110 against 28 in
Salamanca), but it contains radio cells with estimated positions, so one site often appears several
times. We therefore keep Infoantenas as the reference.


7. LIMITATIONS AND NEXT STEPS
-----------------------------
The technology is inferred from the assigned bands, which does not prove that a carrier is on air,
and some bands can be shared between technologies: our evidence for 4G rests on 800, 1800 and 2600
MHz, and 1800 MHz in particular may also carry 5G. The 219 ambiguous RRHs show how much the
technology rule matters for the size of the dataset. The coordinates are those of the register and
were not verified in the field.

The dataset gives us a realistic scenario of 2,085 core RRHs with a wide range of densities. Next we
will inject synthetic RRHs for robustness tests, select the BBU pools with a farthest-first
k-center heuristic, evaluate the delay and the load balancing, and prepare the 5G link budget.

1. OBJECTIVE
------------
The Minimise Delay algorithm [3, section 3.3.3] connects every RRH to the closest BBU pool, provided
the round-trip fronthaul delay stays below the limit, here 200 us. For each RRH we give the list of
pools it can reach and the best one; RRHs that cannot reach any pool are unserved. Pool capacity is not
limited. The results serve to choose the number and position of the pools and are the delay-only
baseline for the load balancing of phase 2.


2. SIMULATION SCENARIO
----------------------
RRHs. Following the review and the thesis (200 sites, 19 pools), we draw 200 of the 2,085 real core
RRHs of week 1 at random (seed 42). A simple random sample keeps the real spatial density: most RRHs
are in the centre and few in the periphery (fig01). The number of real RRHs is a parameter, and the
full set is used as a check in section 6. The program can also add any number of synthetic RRHs,
uniform in the 30 x 30 km box and at least 200 m from any other RRH; they are labelled SYNxxx and drawn
as triangles. The reference run has none.

Traffic patterns. Each RRH is residential, office or mixed. Two modes are available (fig02). In the
random mode the shares are an input (default 50 % mixed, 30 % office, 20 % residential). In the
geographic mode, used as reference, an RRH is office if it lies in a business district of Madrid (the
Castellana axis, AZCA, Cuatro Torres, Distrito Telefonica, Arroyo de la Vega, IFEMA, Julian Camarillo,
Mendez Alvaro, Ciudad Universitaria, Ciudad de la Imagen), mixed if it lies in the historic centre or
at the airport, or if it is isolated (at most 4 real sites within 1 km, i.e. a large cell covering
several land uses, as observed in the thesis for its outer cells), and residential otherwise. This
gives 120 residential, 30 office and 50 mixed RRHs (60 / 15 / 25 %), against 43 / 37 / 20 % in the
thesis [3, table 4.3], where the classes come from measured traffic.

Delay model. With the propagation speed in fibre v = 2e8 m/s [3, section 4.1], the maximum distance
for a round-trip budget is d_max = v RTT / 2 [3, eq. (3.3)], so 200 us gives 20 km. Like the thesis,
we use straight-line distances; a route factor in the configuration can account for longer fibre.

Candidate pools. Every real RRH site is a possible pool site. We select 25 candidates with the
farthest-first k-center heuristic: the first is the RRH nearest the scenario centre, and each next one
is the RRH farthest from those already chosen. The candidates are fixed once (P01 to P25, in selection
order), so adding synthetic RRHs does not move them.


3. ALGORITHMS
-------------
Minimise Delay [3, fig. 3.5], without capacity limits: for each RRH, compute the distance to every
active pool; the pools within d_max are its possible pools; with none the RRH is unserved, with one it
is connected to it, with several it is connected to the nearest.

Number of pools. Starting from the 25 candidates, backward elimination removes one pool at a time,
the one whose removal increases the mean round-trip delay the least, as long as no additional RRH
becomes unserved. The removal order ranks the pools by relevance. For comparison we also evaluate
the first K farthest-first candidates (the k-center solution for K pools). We keep the smallest K
whose mean delay is within 10 % of the 25-pool value.

Categories. For each pool we count the RRHs that (1) can only reach this pool, (2) have it as their
nearest pool but could reach others, and (3) can reach it although another pool is nearer.


4. RESULTS
----------
Number of pools. With 25 pools all 200 RRHs are served, with a mean round-trip delay of 18.2 us
(standard deviation 10.2 us) and a maximum of 37.8 us. Fig06 shows how the delay grows when pools are
removed: 19.9 us with 19 pools, 24.5 us with 12, 29.7 us with 8, 43.3 us with 4 and 71.3 us with a
single central pool. The first pools removed are P25, P11, P10, P07, P06 and P24; with the remaining
19 the mean delay is still within 10 % of the 25-pool value, so we select K = 19, the value of the
thesis and of the review. The farthest-first selection gives the same values at K = 1 and K = 25 but
is clearly worse in between (43.7 us with 8 pools), because it first places pools at the edges of the
area, where there are few RRHs: it minimises the largest distance, not the average.

Delay is not the binding constraint. Even one pool at the centre serves all 200 RRHs at 200 us
(maximum 189 us), because only the corners of the box are more than 20 km from the centre. The
smallest number of pools that serves every RRH is 1 at 200 us, 2 at 150 us, 6 at 100 us and 17 at
50 us (fig07). The choice of the number of pools is therefore driven by the mean delay, by
reliability and by load balancing rather than by the 200 us limit.

Distances. With 19 pools the mean RRH-pool distance is 1.99 km and 99 % of the RRHs are within 5 km
(fig04, fig05); 19 RRHs (9.5 %) are at distance 0 because they host a pool. The distribution is close
to that of the Minimise Delay algorithm in the thesis [3, fig. 4.3], whose mean delay of 23.1 us
(standard deviation 17.6 us) with 19 pools is of the same order as our 19.9 us (11.2 us).

Options per RRH and per pool. At 200 us every RRH can reach 10 to 19 of the 19 pools (mean 16.8,
fig10, fig11), so no RRH depends on a single pool and category 1 is empty (fig08). The assigned RRHs
(category 2) are concentrated in the central pools P19 (33), P01 (32), P16 (18), P15 (16), P13 and
P23 (14 each), while the edge pools P02 and P04 serve 2 RRHs and P03, P05 and P12 serve 3. Over the 25
candidates (fig09), P11 and P25 would serve one RRH each, which confirms their removal. Each pool
can be reached by 130 to 200 RRHs, which leaves a very large freedom for load balancing in phase 2.

Stricter budget. With the same 19 pools and a 50 us budget (5 km), as could be required by future
5G functions, one RRH becomes unserved (RRH1729, 6.8 km from P15; it was covered by the switched-off
P06), 50 RRHs have only one option and the mean is 2.35 options per RRH (fig12). The exclusive RRHs
are around the peripheral pools (P15 has 9, P18 and P21 6 each, P23 5), while the central pools P01,
P19, P13 and P09 have none: there, pools could be moved or merged without losing coverage.


5. ROBUSTNESS
-------------
Keeping the 19 pools fixed, we added 50, 100, 200 and 400 synthetic RRHs and also ran the full set of
2,085 real RRHs (fig13, fig14). At 200 us no RRH is ever unserved. The mean delay rises from 19.9 to
23.5, 25.7, 29.0 and 30.6 us with the synthetic RRHs, because uniformly placed sites fall in areas where
the real network, and hence the pools, are sparse; with the full real set it is 21.7 us. At 50 us the
number of unserved RRHs grows to 12, 20, 40 and 68, almost all synthetic, and to 33 with the full real
set. The automatic choice gives 19 or 20 pools with synthetic RRHs and 17 with the full set. In every
run the program checks that each served RRH uses its nearest pool within d_max, that an RRH is
unserved only when no pool is within d_max, and that the category counts add up; all checks passed.


6. CONCLUSIONS AND NEXT STEPS
-----------------------------
In a 30 x 30 km area a 200 us round-trip budget (20 km of fibre) is not restrictive: a single central
pool would serve every RRH. We select 19 of 25 candidate pools, which keeps the mean delay at 19.9 us
(10 % above the 25-pool value), and every RRH can reach on average 16.8 pools. Delay becomes a real
constraint only for budgets of about 50 us, where the peripheral pools become essential. The limits
are the straight-line distances, the random sample of 200 sites and the approximate geographic rule
for the traffic patterns. In phase 2 we will balance the load with the 19-pool assignment as the
delay-only baseline and compare it with too few (8) and too many (25) pools; the 5G link budget in
Excel is still to be done.
